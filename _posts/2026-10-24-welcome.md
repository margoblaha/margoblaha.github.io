Sometimes following a line of thought leads to an answer, while other times it leads to the discovery that the original question was not quite the right one to ask, and that something I had taken for granted depends on assumptions I had never examined. I find these moments both frustrating and exciting. There is real discomfort in not understanding something, but there is also the possibility of looking again and allowing yourself to change your mind.

This is one reason I have always written: it lets me follow my thoughts more closely, and re-read them many times.

I value the discipline of working with evidence and the usefulness of precise questions, but I have become increasingly interested in what happens around the edges of a scientific explanation, such as the assumptions that exist before a question, the choices involved in deciding what to measure, the interpretations we build on top of results, and the parts of experience that are hard to capture in a language we can access.

Sometimes these questions emerge from just paying more attention to the world around me.

A recent example comes from July 2026. I was on holiday in Puglia, where growing tourism had left many of the places I visited feeling like a facade. Talking with locals, I came away with the impression that this change had been less chosen than imposed on them. It seemed to reach even the food: panino col polpo arrosto, a grilled octopus sandwich, a local dish that now appeared in glamorous reinterpretations, adapted to suit palates that were not local.

Food has always changed, of course. What caught my attention was **who the change was for**.

The observation also opened up questions, like why do so many people today travel the way they do, collecting places as if they were things on a list to tick off? What does it mean for a town to become a postcard, or a landscape a playground? Is this about consumerism, the ways we have learned to want these things from social media, the currently dominant economic system affecting both the tourists and the locals, or is it the interplay between these connected factors? And ultimately where did that leave me, who was, after all, on holiday too?

I don't have just one simple answer, but questions like these make sense to me only when different disciplines have room to talk to each other, like a mycelium beneath the surface linking things that might seem separate.

So here some pieces will grow out of something I have read; others will begin with a conversation, an observation, a personal experience, a thought that keeps returning. Here you will find writing on, for example, gender and bias in science and medicine, how we understand the process of creativity, relationships, and social media and its influence on our perception of the world, and also some of my poetry.

I want to follow ideas across disciplinary boundaries, and to acknowledge that the questions I ask are not merely abstract. 

<span style="display:block; font-size:1.35em; font-style:bold; line-height:1.5; margin:1.5rem 0;">The way we understand the world is intertwined with the way we inhabit it.</span>

Our concepts shape what we notice, what we expect, what we consider possible, and sometimes what we allow ourselves to feel.

This is where I hope the personal dimension of this space will find its place. I observe the world from a position inside it, because my education, experiences, relationships, preferences and assumptions influence which questions occur to me and which answers feel plausible, and thinking carefully may sometimes mean reconsidering something about myself. That is not always easy. It is one thing to value uncertainty in principle and another to sit with it when the stakes feel personal.

I also want to resist the idea that every piece of writing must deliver a definitive insight. There is value in making an argument and supporting it well, but there is also value in documenting a question, tracing a line of reasoning, or admitting that a satisfying answer is, for now, out of reach. So there will be ideas I am still working through and I would like to approach them with care, without requiring them to be resolved before they are allowed to exist here. That is what I want from _Epistemic Margins_: a record of what I am trying to understand, a place where intellectual curiosity can coexist with personal reflection, and where precision does not exclude doubt. The things we do not understand are worth spending time with.

If something here makes you pause, disagree, reconsider an assumption, recognise an experience or follow a question of your own, please get in touch. I am interested in thoughtful disagreement as much as agreement, and in conversations that make an idea more complicated in a functional way.

For now, it is enough to begin.
