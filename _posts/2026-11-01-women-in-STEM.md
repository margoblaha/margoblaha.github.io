Europe says it has a shortage. Under its Digital Decade programme, the Union wants 20 million ICT specialists by 2030, and Eurofound now estimates it will land about five million short. Meanwhile, fewer than one in five of the specialists already employed, 19% in 2024, are women. Eurofound's remedy frames closing the gap as an economic necessity, because without women the EU is drawing on less than three quarters of its potential talent pool.
Read that sentence again. Women appear as untapped inventory. The question the report does not ask is, why does a sector that pays above the European average, and that complains daily of labour scarcity, keep failing to hold on to half the population?
The answer is probably not really about girls' preferences. Maybe it is about the labour model. STEM, in universities and in industry, is organised around an ideal worker who is always available, endlessly mobile, tolerant of insecurity, competitive by temperament and quiet about anything they could complain about. Almost nobody fits that figure. Moreover, the cost of not fitting it is distributed unevenly.

Start with what the European Commission's own statistics say. According to *She Figures 2024*, women are just over a third of researchers in the EU, 34%, and in STEM and ICT fields only about 22% of doctoral graduates. Women outnumber men in higher education overall, so the loss happens along the way in the career.
Then look at the spread across countries. The country sheets in the same report put women at 29% of researchers in Germany, 34% in Sweden (2021 data) and 42 to 43% in Estonia and Portugal. In ICT employment, Eurostat's 2024 figures show women at 27.6% in Estonia, 27.3% in Romania and 27.0% in Bulgaria, against 13.0% in Czechia, 14.7% in Malta and 15.2% in Hungary.

I will not pretend this table explains itself; the causes behind each national figure deserve their own investigation, and richer countries are not simply the fairer ones. But the range is the point. If women's absence from technical work followed from nature, it would not swing by a factor of two across neighbouring labour markets. The gap is made, and therefore can be unmade.

## Who profits from the ideal worker
Follow the pay first. Eurofound reports a gender pay gap of nearly 20% in the wider information and communication sector in 2022, compared with 13% across the economy. Women specialists also report lower satisfaction with pay, less access to paid training and higher work intensity. A 2024 cluster analysis of Eurostat data on EU member states, published in *Studia Europejskie*, suggests the pay gap works as a deterrent to entering the sector rather than a consequence of women's absence. 

Someone captures that differential. When women do more work for less money, with thinner training budgets, the saving appears on an employer's balance sheet as margin. It is a subsidy extracted from the people least able to refuse it.

Now the second mechanism, availability. Sociologists since Joan Acker (1990) have described the ideal worker as someone with no caring responsibilities and total devotion to the job, and Mary Blair-Loy (2003) named the cultural script behind it a work-devotion schema. Hours and responsiveness stand in for commitment because quality is hard to observe. The best large-scale test of what this does comes from the United States, which I include only because I found no European longitudinal equivalent: Cech and Blair-Loy (2019) tracked new parents in STEM and found that 43% of women left full-time STEM work after a first child. So did 23% of new fathers. That figure matters politically. It shows the problem is a job design that treats any life beyond the job as a defect.
 
The European version of this demand is mobility, but the expectation to move between countries and institutions in a research career can function as indirect discrimination against people with caring duties. Mobility for someone with a child, an ageing parent or a partner's job is a toll.
 
Then there is the narrative of shortage itself. Eurofound notes that the share of ICT specialists born outside the EU rose from 9.2% in 2021 to 11.9% in 2024, and that its policy pointers emphasise expanding domestic supply and labour mobility. Notice what is absent: a demand that the sector improve the jobs it already offers to women. Importing workers and publishing campaigns to "attract girls" are cheaper than changing working time, pay structures and supervision.

## Italy and precarity as gender policy
 
Nowhere is the availability norm sharper than in the contracts themselves. Italy shows what happens when a public system runs on insecurity.
 
The PNRR, the national recovery plan financed by European funds, built laboratories and funded fixed-term research posts with a deadline of June 2026. According to CGIL estimates reported by *Il Post*, about 9,000 fixed-term researcher contracts and 23,500 research fellowships (*assegni di ricerca*) expire between 2025 and 2026, and only a little over 2,600 people have been hired with PNRR funds. The same reporting notes that universities have used the *assegno*, a contract tied to a single project, to carry out research at very low cost. Public money bought equipment in the thousands and left the people who operate it on cliffs.
 
The doctoral and postdoctoral association ADI surveyed 2,888 researchers in Italian universities and research centres between April and August 2024. *Avvenire*'s account of the results carries a headline that says it all: we are precarious, we see no future. ADI has also lodged a complaint with the European Commission against two new research contracts, warning that they reintroduce structural precarity. The same survey, per that report, confirms a gender divide alongside a territorial one(??)
 
Why does precarity weigh more heavily on women? Because it is the purest form of the availability demand: no stable income to plan a child around, no certainty about which city you will live in next year, no leverage to refuse overtime. A composite figure helps here, and I mean composite, not a person I interviewed: a biologist in her mid-thirties, on her third fixed-term contract in as many cities, whose partner has stayed behind for a permanent job, who is told that her CV has "gaps". Nothing in her record is weak. The system has simply declared her years of uncertainty to be her own deficiency.
 
That declaration has a name in the psychology literature: impostor syndrome. Critics (Tulshyan and Burey 2021†; Feenstra et al. 2020†) argue the label individualises what is a rational response to an environment that withholds recognition. European data on doctoral candidates point the same way. In a representative Flemish sample of 3,659 PhD students (Levecque et al. 2017), 32% were at risk of a common psychiatric disorder, and the conditions associated with it were organisational: the interface between work and family, job demands and control, supervisors' leadership style, team decision-making culture, and perceptions of career prospects outside academia. The study is cross-sectional, so causation is not established, but it relocates the problem. People are not breaking; they are being broken by management choices.

## Credit, excellence and who gets to count
 
Competition in a scarce system gets rationed through informal judgement, and informal judgement has a history. The construction of "excellence" is itself a set of gendered practices. A US study of research teams found women 13% less likely than men to be credited as authors on articles and 58% less likely on patents (Ross et al. 2022). Survey respondents there also reported that women did more of the conceptualising, data curation and writing, the labour that holds a project together and rarely lands on a patent.
 
Those who profit are the institutions that count outputs and the senior researchers whose names accumulate them. Those who pay are the people whose work is real and uncredited.
 
The technical mastery that is supposedly being measured is itself culturally constructed. Faulkner (2000†) described engineering's "technical/social dualism", in which hard, technical work is prestigious and communicative or relational work is not, and Cech (2013†) argued this ideology contributes to wage inequality in engineering. The work that sustains laboratories, mentoring and coordination, is coded as soft and paid accordingly.
 
## The price of staying quiet
 
Among all this, there is also an implicit norm to be unflappabl to protect everyone else's comfort. Organisational climate is the greatest predictor of sexual harassment in academic science, with male-dominated gender ratios and leadership that signals tolerance among the strongest factors. Where harm is tolerated, speaking up becomes the risky act, so those who name discrimination are cast as the problem.
 
Restraint is not a requirement of this work alone, however I would like to mention ... Ely and Meyerson (2010), studying offshore oil platforms, a setting about as masculine as workplaces get, found that where a safety orientation prevailed, workers admitted limits, learned from mistakes and attended to each other's feelings. Stoicism turned out to be a management culture, not a physical necessity.
 
The standard objection is that inclusion trades off against rigour. The better reply is that narrow, informal criteria (hours worked, confidence displayed, network position) are poor measures of quality. Opening them up is not lowering the bar. It is finally measuring the bar.
 
**1. Treat contracts as gender policy.** The Italian unions and researchers' associations, FLC CGIL and ADI, are making the right demand: stable, permanent posts and an end to project-by-project fellowships. A public system that spends European money on laboratories and then lets the staff expire is not neutral. Funding conditionality, tying public research money to contract quality, would make secure employment a cost of access to the funds, not a favour.
 
**2. Reform what counts.** More than 800 organisations have signed up to the Coalition for Advancing Research Assessment (CoARA), which includes experimenting with narrative CVs that credit mentoring, datasets, software and public engagement. The caution is real: the agreement is not legally binding and progress is self-reported, and whether it changes who gets credit is largely untested. The tool is worth fighting for, and fighting over.
 
**3. Make care and workload visible.** Contribution statements and service-load tracking (Guarino and Borden 2017†, a US study) expose where the invisible work falls. Gender equality plans, now expected of research organisations in Horizon Europe, should be judged by whether they change contracts and workloads, not by whether the paperwork exists. Experience with Athena SWAN charters in the UK (Ovseiko et al. 2017†) is a warning that such schemes can add unpaid labour for the very people they are meant to help.
 
**4. Redesign supervision and teams.** The Flemish findings identify leadership style, decision-making culture and job control as modifiable. A supervisor's behaviour is an institutional choice, and accountability for it can be made a condition of a PI's funding.
 
**5. Build on collective ownership.** Here I move from evidence to proposal, and I say so. Worker cooperatives and democratically governed research collectives, in the tradition of the Basque Mondragon federation, share a structural feature with the best of the oil-platform findings: collective goals, competence defined by the task rather than by performance of toughness, and a say for everyone who does the work. I know of no rigorous study showing cooperative technology firms close the gender gap, and a co-op can reproduce the same hierarchies it was meant to dissolve. But the question deserves experiment, and public procurement and ethical-finance funds could make that experiment possible by favouring cooperative and employee-owned technology providers.
 
**6. Organise.** The gains so far in European labour history came from collective pressure, not from corporate goodwill. Researchers' associations, unions, mutual-aid networks among postdocs and complaint collectives are the only actors whose interests align with changing the model.
 
## The question underneath
 
Europe keeps asking how to get more women into STEM. It should ask what STEM is doing to the people already inside it, and who gains from the answer. A sector that demands availability without limit, pays women less for more intense work, rations credit through informal networks and punishes those who complain does not have a pipeline problem. It has a business model.
 
The shortage is real. It is also, in large part, self-inflicted, and the bill is being sent to workers.
