The alarm goes off at 6:58, because seven o'clock felt a bit lazy. Two glasses of water, because you should hydrate. The notebook with the to-do list, because you should plan your day, and the list has sub-lists. You should work out, ideally before work, ideally weights and not just cardio, because of bone density. You should do your best at work, and also not overwork, and also be visible, and also answer the email from yesterday. You should see your friends, so you open the group chat to find an evening, and the evening becomes a calendar negotiation. You want time with your partner, which is lovely, so you should plan something, prefereably something *intentional*. You should call your mum, ideally before she calls you, and be patient this time and not sigh. Therapy is at 6 p.m., where you should work on that pattern you had as long as you can remember, the one you noticed last month and now notice everywhere. And you should be better at managing your emotions, including the one you are having right now about how many things you should do.

Taken one at a time, none of these thoughts are bad. Moving your body is good. Friends are good. Mums, mostly, are great to call. The trouble is the mountain of "shoulds", not a single one, and the way we beat ourselves up for every one we don't get perfectly right. Each wish shows up as a task followed by a promise that if you get all of this right you might finally be *enough*. Fix the mornings, the body, the friendships, the attachment style, the inner child, and somewhere at the bottom of this long list there is a version of you who is finally perfect and can rest and be loved and be happy.

It is true that we inevitably, and luckily, change as life goes on, and our small habits do matter, but the smuggled story here is that you, as you are this morning, with the unwashed mug from the herbal tea from last evening and the half-crossed-out to-do list, are an imperfect, unfinished, _draft_.

## Self-improvement in public

Social media did not invent by itself this story, just self-improvement used to happen mostly in private before. It was in our diaries and in our changing rooms, and now it happens in public, in numbered days and before-and-after carousels, with perfect meals and daily routines that should keep us young forever.

Have you ever wondered what this does to us? Well, as with most questions, it really depends. When researchers pooled experiments that showed people better-off others on social media, participants on average came away feeling worse about their bodies and themselves, not inspired[^mccomb]. A 2026 meta-analysis covering more than 36,000 people found that habitually comparing upward online goes together with anxiety, low mood and lower self-esteem[^frontiers]. Meanwhile, perfectionism among students in the US, UK and Canada has been climbing since at least 1989, and the fastest-growing kind is *socially prescribed* perfectionism, which is the sense that other people demand flawlessness from you. It began accelerating in the early 2000s[^curran]. The researchers tie it to economic pressure and inequality rather than to our phones, which is a useful corrective. It is good to notice what those pressures sound like from the inside before your life gets swallowed by them. Grades that seem to decide your whole future, jobs that may not exist by the time you qualify, rent that eats half a salary, parents who worry about whether you will make it. They all measure you by what you are not yet, never by what you already are, and social media amplifies this feeling of inadequacy and unfulfillment by showing you, every few minutes, someone who seems to have figured everything out.

## Connection, and who profits from it

The same feeds that sell the 6 a.m. routine also host the group chat for new parents awake at 3 a.m., the forum where someone with a rare diagnosis finally finds the twelve people who understand, the stranger's post that makes you feel less alone. Large studies of adolescents have found the overall link between digital technology use and wellbeing to be negative but small[^orben]. That connection is real, and worth defending.

But it would be naive to call the platforms neutral. They are businesses that sell our attention to advertisers, and we know that they are literally designed to keep us scrolling. Content that stirs us up holds attention better than contentment does, and this came out in 2021 in one analysis of millions of Facebook and Twitter posts found that posts about political opponents were shared about twice as often as posts about one's own side[^rathje]. Envy, outrage and the faint sting of someone else's perfect Sunday is more likely to keep us on the page, while feeling fine sends us off to make dinner. We are not the customers here; we are the product, and the people who profit from our sense of not-yet-enough are not the people feeling it. So the tool can connect us while the business model behind it feeds on insecurities.

## When "I'd like to" becomes "I should"

Most of us can point to some change we are grateful for, like learning to ask for help way before the deadline instead of the day before, to say "I don't know" in a room full of clever people, to remind ourselves to drink water. By the way, some of our growth doesn't look like improvement at all. Like sitting in a therapist's office and noticing you've said "sorry" four times in twenty minutes (for things like "talking too much", LOL). Or like the evening you finally turn your phone face down, notifications off, and feel your shoulders drop for the first time all day.

Our phones made us permanently reachable, and permanent reachability slowly erased the line between being available and being on call. I will maybe digress here, but for "people pleasers" (and I am a recovering one; unlearning it is, I'll admit, my own little self-improvement project) that line was faint to begin with. Saying "no" feels like a small betrayal, so we say yes, and yes, and yes, and then we label it as kindness, but I'll argue that a lot of our "people pleasing" is conflict avoidance masked by compassion. Plus, the "no" can turn into resentment, and resentment is bad for the person carrying it and for the relationship it was supposed to protect. So being unreachable for a few hours is not selfish, but it is part of intimacy, because you cannot fully show up for someone you have already started to resent.

So the trouble is not _improvement_ itself, but when "I'd like to" becomes "I should," and "I should" becomes "I'm not acceptable until." At that point, growth is no longer something you do, but something you owe like a moral duty. 

And I know this. For example, I'm training for a 5 km race, and the plan was to *run today*, but last night I went out for drinks, because friends are also "on the list", and this morning I woke up tired and didn't run. Now I'm writing on the bed trying to let myself rest, and the word my brain keeps handing me is *lazy*, when actually two good things, friendship and training, bumped into each other, and the verdict was that I had failed the latter. The "better version of yourself" is a cruel target because it moves exactly as fast as you do. There is always another habit, another lesson, another layer of healed. Which means that if you keep believing that your worth waits at the finish line, it waits forever, because the ideal version of you is like a horizon which moves when you walk toward it. I don't say this to be discouraging, because walking is still worth it; the view changes, and you change with it. The trap is not the walking, but the belief that you are only allowed to rest, or to be loved, once you get "there". So here is what I'm trying to believe: **it is really okay to be lazy sometimes**.

## We are not projects

Here is the reversal I keep coming back to. **We are not projects.** A project is defined by its deliverable, and its value lives in the finished state; a person's value does _not_. Love that arrives only once the optimisation is complete is not love _for you_, but _validation of the output_ (in the modeller’s sense), a check that you now match the expected result.

This is where self-compassion is such a gift that you can give to yourself. Psychologists describe it as meeting your own failures with kindness instead of judgment, remembering that failing is what humans do, and noticing pain without either denying it or drowning in it[^neff]. In surveys of adolescents and adults, self-compassion weakened the link between perfectionism and depression[^ferrari], and it does not, as we fear, kill motivation. In experiments, people prompted to be self-compassionate about a failure studied longer for a retest and felt more motivated to make amends for a wrong[^breines]. The kindness was the soil for growth, not the reward after it.

## We don't become ourselves alone

The self-help shelf may be stocked for a solo repair job (your mindset, your habits, your boundaries), as if a person were a bicycle you could fix in the garage, but people grow and change in relationship with others. Attachment theory describes a "secure base", which is someone to return to, whose reliability is what makes venturing out easier. Its "adult version" has been called the *dependency paradox*, where in studies of couples, people whose partners accepted their need for support grew more independent over time, not less[^feeney]. Leaning on someone, it turns out, is often how we learn to stand.

The stakes are not only emotional. Across 148 studies and more than 300,000 people, those with stronger social ties had about 50 percent higher odds of survival over the study periods, an effect comparable to familiar risk factors like smoking[^holt]. Quality matters, though: across 126 studies, better marriages were modestly but consistently linked to better health[^robles]. The best relationships let us be _known_, which is different from being _impressive_, and let us be chosen on a day when we have nothing to show for ourselves.

## Rupture and repair

Let's be careful though: "find a secure partner" slides easily onto the to-do list. Love does not dissolve trauma, replace therapy, nor it excuses us from noticing what we do to other people. A relationship turned into a project, audited for red flags, measured against an ideal attachment style, endlessly "worked on," becomes one more place where nobody is allowed to rest. Most of the times, if you love people, you will hurt them. Not because there is something wrong with you and with them, but because you two work differently, and you are both people with feelings, and neither of you can see all the way inside the other (spoiler: nobody can! Please allow me to digress. Philosophers call it _the problem of other minds_: I can never feel your sadness directly, only guess at it from your face, your words, the way you go quiet. Wittgenstein imagined everyone carrying a box with a "beetle" inside that no one else is allowed to look into. We all talk about our beetles anyway, confidently, as if we were describing the same creature. Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat and concluded that, from the outside, we actually cannot really know. I think about this often, as if a bat is out of reach can also be the person asleep next to you. We will never understand another person completely, because we have not lived their life. What we can do is understand each other enough, through the inherently limited languages we share. End of detour. This one could easily be an essay of its own, so consider it a trailer. Stay tuned.) So, say you need to talk it through tonight; they need to sleep on it. You show love by doing things; they just wanted you to sit down and listen. You were joking; they were tired. You forgot the appointment that mattered. You said the true thing at the wrong moment, or the wrong thing because your own day had been long. You pulled away when they reached for you, or reached for them when they needed room. And they will hurt you too, in the same ordinary ways. Someone will say that it hurt, and the other will feel the urge to explain why it shouldn't have. And then...

It's _okay_.

You are both imperfect, both feeling things, both doing this without a manual, and neither is ever going to get it right every time anyways, and nobody needs to.

The optimization mindset reads all of this as failure, proof that the work is unfinished. A developmental science study actually observed mothers and babies at play, and psychologists found that even healthy pairs spent most of their time slightly out of sync[^tronick]. What mattered was the _repair_ afterwards. Closeness is, indeed, the habit of returning to each other, more than the absence of rupture.
Returning, however, costs us our vulnerability, because it means being able to stand inside your imperfection while someone you love looks straight at it.

The reverse is true as well. Letting someone love you means handing them the power to hurt you, but all this vulnerability is not a design flaw in intimacy , but its condition. The only person who cannot be hurt is the one no one can reach.

## _Sorry about the mess_

So perhaps the goal was never to be finished with our project; maybe just to be more honest and more willing to stay human with other humans. 

Most of us have a sentence we say at the door when friends come over: *sorry about the mess.* We learnt it from other and we tend to say it whether or not there is a mess, as if the place should have been ready and we failed to finish it in time. Have you noticed that the friends who matter always do the same thing? They just step over the shoes, probably look inside your fridge, and sit down anywhere. They just came to talk, to drink tea, to eat your snacks and to be with you for a while.

Then one of them picks something up off the couch. A comic you drew this afternoon, still lying there next to a pen without its cap, half-buried in blankets you pulled over yourself because you were a little cold and wanted to be cozy while you drew. *What on earth is this??* they ask, and you realize the mess was, is, all you, too. Including all the tangled blankets, the uncapped pen, the small, silly, thing you made just because you wanted to.

We spend so long getting ready for company, but the people who love us were never waiting for the house, and for us, to be perfect.
