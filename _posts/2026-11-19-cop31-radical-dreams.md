*COP31 and radical dreams.*

## A stab

It's the second week of COP31, I am reading an interview with Italy's minister for the environment and energy security. He gave it to the young reporters of Stampa Giovanile, the youth press agency I travelled with to Belém's COP30 last year. If the war in the Gulf drags on and gas climbs above €100, he told them, he may have to order Italy's coal plants back on. It would be, he said, "una coltellata"[^src1], a stab. To himself, I think, to the man who had proudly closed those plants. Isn't that a curious place to feel the blade when the people who would breathe the smoke are somewhere else?
 
The day before, the summit's own draft text had asked the world for "accelerated action to phase out fossil fuels"[^src2]. And you may have wondered what is the point of a room where everyone agrees the house is on fire, and a good number of the guests sell matches?

Part of me doesn't want a better COP, part of me wants to burn the furniture. (metaphorically, of course!) I want a world where the people selling matches aren't invited to plan the fire brigade and where "growth" isn't the god every delegation has to genuflect to before talking. I'm an idealist and _maybe_ it is a phase, as I've heard from others, but it has been a _long phase_.

I have been dreaming of a radically different society while taking seriously the slow and sometimes deeply unglamorous room we actually have. Change mostly happens one step at a time, unless we manage to **destroy ourselves first**, which is, let's say, not so unlikely.

In 1970, an Italian feminist manifesto proposed, quite literally, to _spit on Hegel_,[^lonzi] because a grand philosophy of History written without women had no business telling women where history was going. I'd like to extend the courtesy to any grand theory of "how the world works" that was written without the people drowning in it.

## The world as it is (spoiler: on fire)
 
A **COP** is the annual **Conference of the Parties** to the 1992 UN climate convention. In Antalya there are 198 Parties[^src3]: 197 states plus the European Union, which first has to agree with its own 27 members. (If you have ever organised a dinner for 27 people, you already understand the EU.) Anna Pelicci, who heads the Italian Climate Network's delegation, described the negotiations as a _clock_[^src4], where each Party is a gear, and when one turns, the others are forced to take a position.
 
Last year I learnt that, in practice, co-chairs draft compromise texts, delegations fight over them line by line, sometimes over a single verb, and the knottiest disputes migrate to evening "informal informals". Yes, that is the real name. Because the Parties never agreed on voting rules, everything needs _consensus_. What can such a machine do? It can set shared goals, require every country to file a climate plan every five years, create funds and name things (which in diplomacy is half of making them real). What can't it do? Legislate, tax, enforce, or switch off a single coal plant.
 
Physics is definitely not to be negotiated, and UNEP's 2026 Emissions Gap Report concludes that keeping warming to 1.5°C without overshoot is no longer possible[^src5], as full implementation of national plans points to about 2.6°C, current policies to 2.8°C. So the door to never crossing 1.5°C has been closed for good[^src6], and what about the damage? The 2026 Lancet Countdown links 710,000 deaths in 2023 to heat[^src7], more than 70% of them attributable to human-caused warming.
 
What you can't find in studies like these is who should cut, who should pay, and how fast, because that's politics, and in Antalya politics looks like this.  \*clears throat.\*

The 18-page draft calls for faster action to phase out fossil fuels, while the Arab Group opposes any reference[^src8] to the fossil-fuel roadmaps that more than 80 countries failed to get into the Belém deal[^src9]. The draft proposes at least $120 billion a year[^src10] for adaptation by 2035, against needs of around $305 billion[^src11], while donors offered the Adaptation Fund about $50 million, a sixth of its own floor[^src12]. The Kick Big Polluters Out coalition counted 103 polluting companies and trade groups[^src13] whose delegations outnumber Nepal's six to one. Italy, according to Fossil Free Politics, brought about 50 fossil-industry representatives[^src14] but no head of government. And war is the elephant in the room[^src15]: Russia's invasion of Ukraine _alone_ has produced an estimated 311 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent in four years, roughly France's annual emissions.

Yeah, breathe. Maybe read that again.
 
Then try to picture yourself as a delegate from a smaller country. Would it feel like a negotiation, or like an audience? It might be true that attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity,[^weil] and on a good day, a COP is a machine for forcing attention, because it makes a minister from Rome sit across from Pacific delegates who fear their islands will go under, and listen, at least for the length of a meeting. On a bad day, it's a machine for performing attention while looking at your phone.
 
## Spooks and rifts
 
A COP negotiates the *speed* of the problem, debating how fast to phase out fossil fuels, but not the economy built on burning them.
 
Why? Because those questions lead straight to the engine room. Long before anyone measured CO₂, critics of capitalism described a "rift" in the metabolism between society and the soil, opened by an economy that takes from nature faster than nature can give back.[^marx] That rift has now gone global, and it's no accident, as capitalism was born from *enclosure*, the fencing-off of commons that people used to share,[^federici] and it keeps feeding on what it cannot replace, from nature to care work to public power itself. Hence its nickname, *cannibal capitalism*.[^fraser] Seen this way, a fossil-fuel lobbyist at a climate summit isn't a contradiction; it's the system eating lunch.
 
The same system also furnishes the room. Growth, markets, the nation-state and "the national interest" sit there a bit too comfortably and nobody asks whether they could be, _maybe_, moved? They work a bit like *spooks*,[^stirner] abstractions we obey as if they were real masters, particular interests passed off as the "benefit of all". "The national interest" is the perfect spook, because nobody can really tell you exactly what it is, yet it can keep a coal plant open.
 
What about the house rules? Power at a COP isn't only where the gavel is, as I learnt last year as an observer at COP30. It truly permeates everything: the reporting templates, the accounting rules, the "informal informals" and the colour of your badge, in the "conduct of conduct" (yep, that's the real term).[^foucault] The same structure that makes countries file their plans also decides what counts as a problem worth addressing. Military emissions, for instance, are (un?)surprisingly still largely left out[^src16]. However, where there is power, there is resistance,[^foucault2] and every template can be turned against its makers.
 
You can't put out a fire by handing matches to the people who started it.[^vandamme] The room isn't even democratic: consensus works like a veto for anyone stubborn enough to use it, and fossil-fuel exporters blocked voting rules at the very first COP in 1995[^src17]. Meanwhile the presidency's AI pledge[^src18] is "voluntary and non-prescriptive", with no targets and not one mention of coal, oil or gas. A "bollino verde", as Jill McArdle of Beyond Fossil Fuels put it.[^src18]
 
So here's a question for you: is "realism" a description of the world, or a decision about it?
 
What do I dream of instead? Not a detailed master plan, as those who think they've drawn the perfect society for everyone else have a worrying track record. Maybe the root problem is a set of dualisms, like reason over nature, man over woman, human over everything else, that turn the living world into background scenery.[^plumwood] Let's reject the binary! (And not only in climate policy. I've spent enough of my life being asked to pick a side to know the best answer is usually "Yeah, both. Both is good.") So, I dream of an economy organised around *enough* rather than *more*, one that measures success by how well we live rather than by how much we produce, of a world that remembers who burned the most, for longest, and got rich doing it, which is what "common but differentiated responsibilities" means when you take it seriously instead of reciting it, and a politics where those who flood first get to have a real say in things. Back in 1792, the demand was for women to have power not over men, "but over themselves".[^wollstonecraft] Swap "women" for "small island states" and you have the most reasonable demand at COP31.
 
I'd push it further down, into the soil. Beware the "monocultures of the mind":[^shiva] ways of thinking that see a forest as timber and a field as yield. The alternative is an Earth Democracy that puts decisions closest to the soil and to the people who live from it. Tellingly, the COP31 draft cover decision contains no dedicated provisions on agriculture and food systems[^src19]. The soil, once again, didn't get a badge.
 
Can you negotiate your way out of a system with the people who profit most from it?
 
## No gods, no masters, some compromises
 
History mostly sides with the pragmatists, because big dreams usually arrive in tiny stubborn pieces, usually pushed by the least powerful players in the room. Vulnerable countries demanded a loss and damage fund for about three decades before COP27 finally created it. Small island states refused to sign anything weaker than 1.5°C, which is why the goal is in the Paris Agreement. A campaign started by law students in the Pacific was taken to the UN by Vanuatu[^src20], and in 2025 it produced a world-court opinion finding that failing to rein in fossil-fuel production, consumption or subsidies may be an internationally wrongful act[^src21]. None of these was a revolution. Each one changed what the next step could be.
 
Even the _patron saint of revolutions_ knew that people make their own history, but under circumstances "transmitted from the past".[^marx2] Antalya is one of those circumstances. As Eleonora Cogo told Stampa Giovanile last year, a good agreement is one where most parties are a little disappointed, because it means everyone had to compromise, which is the price of deciding anything with people who aren't you, but is not the same as surrender or defeat. Some believe that no revolution can liberate unless its *means* are identical in spirit to its *ends*.[^goldman] So the small steps are where the dream gets tested. A transition that tramples workers, or Indigenous land in the rush for critical minerals, isn't a step towards a just world, just the old world with solar panels.
 
And how do you tell a real step from a cosmetic one? Separate reforms that just make a system more bearable from *non-reformist reforms*,[^gorz] which are changes that _shift who holds power_, and so make the next change possible. A voluntary pledge with no targets is the first kind. Majority voting, kicking polluters out of the room, reparations backed by international law and a loss and damage fund that actually pays are the second.
 
And the test applies to us, too. For structural injustices like climate change, responsibility falls on everyone whose everyday actions keep the structure running, scaled to their power, privilege and capacity to act together.[^young] Which means it also falls a little bit on me and on _you_. The good news is that nobody has to carry it alone, or all at once. Commons, as a whole career of fieldwork has shown, rarely get saved by one grand treaty.[^ostrom] They get saved by many centres of action, such as rules the people involved help write, sanctions that escalate slowly, and people talking to each other far more than seems efficient. A COP is one node of that messy, many-centred system, together with courts, cities, unions, movements and, maybe, even blogs. (Look, I'm trying here.)
 
So this is the question I now ask of every COP headline. Not "is it enough?", because it never is. Just, does it move power, even a little?
 
## Socialism or barbarism (climate mix edition)
 
There is, of course, a third path between revolution and reform, and it's the one people usually do not campaign for: **collapse**. _Seven_ of nine planetary boundaries are already crossed[^src22]. Scientists in Antalya warned that crossing certain thresholds would trigger feedbacks that amplify warming on their own[^src23]. UNEP estimates that every five years of emissions at current levels adds about 0.1°C[^src24] to peak warming.
 
I am just reporting, so you may, or may not, agree: in 1915, a revolutionary writing from prison said humanity faced a choice betwween socialism and barbarism.[^luxemburg] You can argue about the first one for days, while the second looks less like rhetoric every year. Keeping the status quo is not an option, because the world will change either way. What we can still choose is whether that change is one we design, or one we simply suffer.

Politics, as a sociologist who was no romantic put it, is "the strong and slow boring of hard boards", and yet people would never have reached the possible if they hadn't kept reaching for the impossible.[^weber] The attitude I'm after is "staying with the trouble",[^haraway] so refusing both the fantasy of a clean fix and the cosy despair of "it's too late anyway". **Despair, after all, is just another way of not showing up.**
 
And who holds the drill? If we wanted to punish governments that knowingly wreck the commons, we have some tools, like international courts, and trade penalties modelled as "climate clubs".[^nordhaus] But if you count emissions from 1850, the worst offenders are the very countries that control the markets you'd punish with. Developing countries already call Europe's carbon border levy coercive[^src25]. Punishment designed by the powerful has a habit of landing on the weak, and are we ready to punish deliberate damage even if it means being judged for history too?
 
## Pessimismo dell'intelligenza, ottimismo della volontà[^gramsci]
 
The intellect looks at Antalya and keeps its expectations low. COP31 closes tomorrow, Friday 20 November, and the text will change before then. Realistically, success would be an adaptation finance number with a deadline, a Just Transition Mechanism with working rules, and "phase out" surviving into the final text instead of fading into "transition" or "note".
 
So what does the COP make possible? This is where Pelicci's clock comes back. A clock doesn't decide what time it is, and the Lancet's death counts and UNEP's degrees are not debatable. What a COP offers is one place where 198 governments, with every reason to distrust each other, have to read the same time together.
 
Reading the time isn't the same as acting on it, of course; nevertheless, a cover decision moves the world, slowly, and only through the thousands of hands that decide to act on it. Maybe that's what actual power is:[^arendt] not something you own, but something that springs up between people when they act together, and vanishes when they scatter. Revolutions need it too.
 
So I'll keep both: the dream of a different society, and the patience for a cover decision. COPs are not the revolution, but deciding together, with people we don't trust, about a planet we can't divide, might be the only road that still leads there.
 
What's the smallest step you could take tomorrow toward the world you actually want? And does it move power, even a little?
